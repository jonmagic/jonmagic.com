Who’s running

Statewide offices plus U.S. House District 15, Assembly District 21 and Board of Equalization District 2. Check district races against your sample ballot. D means Democratic; R means Republican.

State Assembly, District 21

Diane Papan (D) vs. Jabra J. Muhawieh (R)

Diane Papan is the incumbent assemblymember. Jabra J. Muhawieh is an enrolled agent and businessman. Confirm your Assembly district with the county before using this comparison.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Governor

Xavier Becerra (D) vs. Steve Hilton (R)

Becerra supports state financing for affordable housing and a progressive income tax. He supports clean-energy goals but is open to revising them over affordability concerns. Hilton favors tax cuts, fewer development restrictions and rolling back climate mandates. Both oppose Proposition 40.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Lieutenant Governor

Fiona Ma (D) vs. Gloria Romero (R)

Ma is the current state treasurer and emphasizes long state-government experience. Romero, a former Democratic state Senate majority leader who became a Republican in 2024, differs notably on school choice, COVID-era policy and gender-identity issues.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Secretary of State

Shirley N. Weber (D) vs. Donald P. Wagner (R)

Weber, the incumbent, emphasizes voting access, transparency and cybersecurity. Wagner supports voter-ID requirements and has criticized the speed of California's ballot counting.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Controller

Malia Cohen (D) vs. Herb Morgan (R)

Cohen is the incumbent chief accountant and emphasizes fiscal caution, transparency and administration. Morgan is an investment executive running principally on fraud/waste detection and independent oversight.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Treasurer

Eleni Kounalakis (D) vs. Jennifer Hawks (R)

Kounalakis is the lieutenant governor and points to extensive state-board experience; Hawks is a retired private-sector executive who argues that not having held elected office would make her a more independent check on state government.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Attorney General

Rob Bonta (D) vs. Michael Gates (R)

Bonta has used the office to enforce state housing and criminal-justice laws and challenge federal Trump-administration policies. Gates, formerly Huntington Beach city attorney and later a federal DOJ official, has challenged several California housing and criminal-justice policies.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Insurance Commissioner

Ben Allen (D) vs. Jane Kim (D)

Allen emphasizes wildfire mitigation, stronger Insurance Department staffing/enforcement and tighter FAIR Plan oversight. Kim proposes a state-backed “natural disaster insurance for all” system, expanded low-cost auto coverage and stronger penalties/transparency around claims.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Board of Equalization, District 2

Sally Lieber (D) vs. John Pimentel (D)

Lieber is the incumbent; Pimentel is a San Mateo County Community College District trustee. This office administers parts of California's tax system.

Read the CalMatters guide →

Superintendent of Public Instruction

Richard Barrera vs. Sonja Shaw — nonpartisan

Barrera emphasizes school funding, early-childhood education and the teacher pipeline. Shaw emphasizes academic basics, parental authority and opposition to some transgender-student policies. A new law also transfers significant Department of Education authority away from this office beginning in 2027.

Read the CalMatters guide →

For school boards, city council and other local offices, use the county candidate roster and your sample ballot. An uncontested seat or a district on a different election cycle may not appear. This guide does not infer those omissions from your ZIP code.

03 / Close to home

Nine local measures to understand

City, county, transit and school measures from the original San Mateo research. Match each one to your ballot. These summaries describe what approval would change; a no vote rejects the proposal.

San Mateo H — sales tax

Raises San Mateo's city transactions/use tax by ¼ percentage point, effectively taking the city's local component from 0.25% to 0.50%. The city estimates roughly $7 million/year. It is a general tax rather than money legally restricted to one program, has local auditing/oversight, and does not have a sunset date. A NO leaves the current city tax unchanged.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

San Mateo F — charter amendments

Makes a package of city-charter procedural changes: among them, extending certain council-vacancy deadlines from 30 to 60 days, modernizing meeting/publication/procurement language and conforming the charter to district elections. This is mostly a governance/administrative measure rather than a tax measure.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

County L — extreme weather

Adds charter language making long-term resilience to fire, flooding, heat, drought, storms and sea-level rise a county priority. It does not by itself levy a tax or appropriate a specific amount.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

County AA — affirmation of rights

Adds charter language affirming dignity, civil rights, equal protection, equity, inclusion, accessibility and opportunity. It similarly changes the county charter rather than establishing a specific spending program.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

County J — vacancies

Extends the Board of Supervisors' window for filling certain elected-office vacancies from 30 to 60 days and adds a noticed public-meeting/comment requirement before an appointment.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

County U — redistricting

Moves the drawing of the county's five supervisor districts after each census from the Board of Supervisors to an independent redistricting commission. NBC Bay Area also highlights this as one of San Mateo County's notable measures.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

Regional Transit Measure (RTM)

Adds a 0.5% sales tax in San Mateo County (1% in San Francisco) for 14 years. It is projected to raise about $980 million annually regionwide, primarily supporting BART, Muni, Caltrain, AC Transit and other transit, along with rider programs and some county transportation/road projects.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

Measure V — Community College bond

Authorizes $848 million in San Mateo County Community College District bonds for facilities, technology, safety and infrastructure. The official tax-rate estimate reaches roughly $18 per $100,000 of assessed value, with about $1.35 billion estimated total principal-and-interest repayment. Requires 55%.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

Measure W — SMFCSD bond

Authorizes $498 million for San Mateo-Foster City school facilities: classrooms/STEM spaces, roofs/windows, emergency systems, accessibility/security and teacher/staff housing among the listed uses. Estimated average tax is about $29 per $100,000 of assessed value, with roughly $900 million total repayment including interest. Requires 55%.

County measure text, analysis and arguments →

For school bonds, assessed value means the value on your property-tax assessment, which can differ substantially from market value. The tax figures above are estimates, not a quote for your property.

04 / Across California

The 14 state propositions

A quick explanation of what a yes vote would do. A no vote rejects that proposition. Follow the official guide for the full text, fiscal analysis, and arguments on both sides.

Proposition 1 — Housing bond

A yes vote would borrow $11.25B for affordable rental housing, supportive housing, veterans' mortgages, homeownership assistance and related programs.

Legislative Analyst estimates $500–600M/year for ~25 years to repay it.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 2 — Rainy-day fund

A yes vote would raise the maximum state reserve from 10% to 20% of General Fund tax revenue and permit money to be used for California's federal unemployment-insurance debt.

The policy question is larger fiscal reserves versus keeping more revenue subject to existing spending/refund rules.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 3 — High-income tax

A yes vote would make permanent the higher state income-tax rates on high earners that currently expire, with revenue supporting schools and healthcare.

It converts what has been a temporary/extended tax into a permanent one.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 4 — Campaign finance

A yes vote would remove California's prohibition on state/local public financing of election campaigns, allowing governments to create such systems.

It authorizes public financing; it does not itself create one statewide.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 5 — Recall elections

A yes vote would separate the recall question from the election of a permanent successor; a temporary replacement would serve until the later election, and the recalled officeholder could run in it.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 37 — Homebuyer loans

A yes vote would authorize up to $25B in revenue bonds for fixed-rate secondary mortgages of up to 17% of qualifying newly built homes, with borrowers providing at least 3% down.

Bonds are repaid from borrowers' mortgage payments; the state analysis says no direct state/local cost.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 38 — Immunology bond

A yes vote would borrow $8.4B for immunology/immunotherapy research.

LAO estimates about $500M/year for 25 years. CalMatters found that the criteria for roughly half the funding appear to leave one UCLA-affiliated nonprofit as the current qualifying institution; the campaign says the detailed criteria are intended to ensure a research institute can begin high-quality work immediately.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 39 — Voter ID

A yes vote would require government-issued ID for in-person voting and identifying information for mail voting.

This would materially change California's current voter-identification rules.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 40 — Billionaire tax

A yes vote would impose a one-time wealth tax on qualifying billionaires, primarily to fund healthcare, with some funding for education and food assistance.

Official fiscal analysis anticipates tens of billions temporarily, while also noting possible ongoing income-tax revenue losses if billionaires leave or change residency.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 41 — Tax/spending rules

A yes vote would require audits of new special-tax programs and generally make new-tax revenue subject to the state spending limit.

It was drafted partly in response to Prop. 40.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 42 — Personal-property taxes

A yes vote would constitutionally bar new state taxes based merely on ownership of personal property/assets and certain retroactive taxes.

Propositions 40, 41 and 42 contain potentially conflicting provisions. Read their official analyses together before deciding.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 43 — local tax threshold

A yes vote would raise the approval threshold for citizen-initiated local special taxes from a majority to two-thirds, matching the threshold applicable to local-government-initiated special taxes.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 44 — community clinics

A yes vote would require nonprofit federally qualified health centers to spend 90% of revenue on “program services” and allow penalties for falling below the requirement.

Supporters frame it as patient-care accountability; clinic organizations argue the definition/penalties could reduce services or force closures.

Official analysis and arguments →

Proposition 45 — CEQA review

A yes vote would impose faster environmental-review timelines for specified housing, transportation, water, health and clean-energy projects and limit some judicial delays.

The central policy tension is faster permitting versus retaining the existing scope of environmental review/litigation.

Official analysis and arguments →
